Research

Everyday listening rarely happens in quiet. Our research asks how listeners pull a single voice out of a noisy background, how that ability differs across languages and clinical populations, and what role cognition plays when listening becomes difficult.

Speech

Speech perception in multi-talker environments

When several people speak at once, listeners rely on differences between voices — where a talker is located, how high or low the voice is, how the speech unfolds over time — to follow the one that matters. We measure how much benefit these cues provide, how that benefit changes as competing talkers are added, and why some listeners gain far more from them than others.

Cross-language and cross-linguistic listening

Most of what we know about speech in noise comes from English. We test whether those findings generalize by studying listeners of Mandarin Chinese, Spanish, and English, including tone languages in which pitch carries word meaning. The question is which aspects of listening in noise are universal properties of the auditory system, and which depend on a listener's language experience.

Hearing loss and hearing devices

Hearing loss and cochlear implantation reshape the signal a listener has to work with. We study how device users perceive speech, how stimulation can be tailored to reduce unwanted side effects, and how children and adults with cochlear implants perform relative to peers with typical hearing.

Speech denoising using deep learning

Removing noise from a recording is not the same as making it easier to understand. We develop and evaluate deep neural networks for speech enhancement and speaker separation, working on models that are efficient and robust enough for real devices — and we test their output with human listeners, because intelligibility, not a signal-processing score, is what ultimately matters.

Tinnitus

Tinnitus — the perception of sound without an external source — is common, poorly understood, and difficult to treat. We work on both halves of the problem: what gives rise to the percept and its downstream costs, and what can actually be done to relieve them.

Mechanisms

Tinnitus rarely arrives alone. It often accompanies hearing loss, and many people with tinnitus report difficulty concentrating and following conversation in noisy rooms — difficulties that are not explained by the audiogram. We ask what links the percept to these everyday costs.

One line of work compares the cognitive profile of listeners with tinnitus to that of people with other attention-related conditions, testing whether the same cognitive factors predict speech-in-babble recognition across groups. If the predictors are shared, the listening difficulty in tinnitus may be an attentional problem as much as an auditory one. A second line uses EEG to characterize how the brain responds to different noise backgrounds, applying machine-learning analysis to identify the spectral and spatial patterns that distinguish them.

Intervention

Our main intervention project asks whether nicotine, a cholinergic agent that sharpens auditory attention, can reduce tinnitus and improve the cognitive and speech performance that tinnitus disrupts. We are examining dosage-dependent effects on both tinnitus perception and listening performance. This work is supported by a 2026 SDSU Seed Grant from the Division of Research and Innovation.

We also work on tinnitus that is caused or worsened by hearing devices. Cochlear implantation can exacerbate tinnitus in the opposite ear, and we develop customized stimulation strategies that reduce this and other side effects without sacrificing speech understanding — an approach aimed at what clinicians can change in the clinic, for patients who have already been implanted.

Cognition

Cognitive function in neurotypical adults and in neurological disorders

Listening in noise draws on attention, memory, and processing speed as well as on the ear. We examine how cognitive performance predicts individual differences in speech recognition among neurotypical adults, and we develop automated acoustic and linguistic measures of spontaneous speech that may help screen for cognitive impairment and neurological conditions such as traumatic brain injury.

Methods

Our studies combine psychoacoustic and speech-perception testing, standardized cognitive assessment, acoustic and linguistic analysis of recorded speech, electroencephalography (EEG), and machine-learning analysis of speech and neural data.
