Research

Everyday listening rarely happens in quiet. Our research asks how listeners pull a single voice out of a noisy background, how that ability differs across languages and clinical populations, and what role cognition plays when listening becomes difficult.

Speech perception in multi-talker environments

When several people speak at once, listeners rely on differences between voices — where a talker is located in space, how high or low their voice is, and how their speech unfolds over time — to follow the one that matters. We measure how much benefit these cues provide, how that benefit changes as the number of competing talkers grows, and why some listeners gain far more from them than others.

Cross-language and cross-linguistic listening

Most of what we know about speech in noise comes from English. We test whether those findings generalize by studying listeners of Mandarin Chinese, Spanish, and English, including tone languages in which pitch carries word meaning. This work asks which aspects of listening in noise are universal properties of the auditory system and which depend on a listener's language experience.

Tinnitus

Tinnitus — the perception of sound without an external source — is common, poorly understood, and difficult to treat. Our current work asks whether nicotine can alleviate tinnitus and the attention and processing difficulties that often accompany it, and examines the cognitive predictors that tinnitus shares with other attentional conditions. This work is supported by a 2026 SDSU Seed Grant from the Division of Research and Innovation.

Hearing loss and hearing devices

Hearing loss and cochlear implantation reshape the signal a listener has to work with. We study how device users perceive speech, how stimulation can be customized to reduce unwanted side effects — including tinnitus exacerbated by implantation — and how children and adults with cochlear implants perform on speech recognition tasks relative to peers with typical hearing.

Cognition, aging, and speech-based clinical measures

Listening in noise draws on attention, memory, and processing speed as well as on the ear. We examine how cognitive performance predicts individual differences in speech recognition, and we develop automated acoustic and linguistic measures of spontaneous speech that may help screen for cognitive impairment and neurological conditions such as traumatic brain injury.

Methods

Our studies combine psychoacoustic and speech-perception testing, standardized cognitive assessment, acoustic and linguistic analysis of recorded speech, electroencephalography (EEG), and machine-learning analysis of speech and neural data.
